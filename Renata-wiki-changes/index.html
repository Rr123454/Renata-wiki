01 / A question worth exploring

Life after
gallbladder surgery.

For some people, symptoms continue after gallbladder removal. This broad group of symptoms is known as post-cholecystectomy syndrome (PCS).

Our story begins with one part of that picture: how bile acids move through the body.

02 / The starting point

A reservoir.
A rhythm.

The liver makes bile. The gallbladder, tucked beneath it, stores bile and releases it to help digest food.

After removal, bile travels from the liver directly to the small intestine. The storage step is gone; the pattern of bile flow changes.

Background: NIDDK ↗

The gallbladder stores bile between meals.

03 / The human side

When symptoms
stay behind.

Most people can live normally without a gallbladder. Some experience ongoing abdominal discomfort, digestive symptoms, or diarrhea.

PCS has several possible causes. Renata focuses on a research question about bile-acid chemistry—not a single explanation for every patient’s symptoms.

04 / A molecular helping hand

A small addition.
A different molecule.

The human enzyme SULT2A1 helps sulfate bile acids such as lithocholic acid (LCA).

Using the sulfate donor PAPS, the enzyme adds a sulfate group. This can increase water solubility and support elimination—a route involved in bile-acid detoxification.

Human liver LCA sulfation ↗

05 / A question of balance

Useful chemistry.
Too much exposure.

LCA and DCA are secondary bile acids formed through microbial metabolism. At excessive concentrations, these hydrophobic molecules can damage cells.

Changing bile-acid exposure motivates our question: could an engineered bacterial enzyme help transform LCA into its sulfated form?

We are investigating that possibility. Clinical benefit in PCS has not been established.

06 / Reimagine the fit

Meet BtSULT.
Imagine SulFlux.

BtSULT is a sulfotransferase from Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron. Its substrate preferences make it an interesting starting point, but native BtSULT is not an established route for efficient LCA sulfation.

Renata is exploring changes to the enzyme’s binding pocket. SulFlux is the name of our proposed engineered design.

07 / From blueprint to possibility

A living system.
A research vision.

Our proposed system would place the enzyme’s genetic blueprint in an E. coli chassis, alongside the machinery needed for sulfate donation.

A future probiotic concept motivates the work. Establishing expression, LCA conversion, safety, and benefit remains part of the research ahead.
