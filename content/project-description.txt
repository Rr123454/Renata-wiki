The gallbladder is a small organ located beneath the liver that stores and concentrates bile, a digestive fluid essential for breaking down and absorbing dietary fats. The liver continuously produces bile, which contains bile acids synthesized from cholesterol. During digestion, the gallbladder releases concentrated bile into the small intestine, helping the body absorb fats and fat-soluble nutrients.
Most bile acids are not eliminated after a single digestive cycle. Approximately 95% are reabsorbed, primarily in the terminal ileum, and returned to the liver through a process known as enterohepatic circulation. This system allows the body to recycle bile acids repeatedly, maintaining an efficient digestive process. [1]
Gallstones and other gallbladder disorders can interfere with this process, often causing significant abdominal pain and digestive complications. A common treatment is cholecystectomy, the surgical removal of the gallbladder. In 2019, approximately 935,000 cholecystectomies were performed in the United States, making it one of the country's most common gastrointestinal surgical procedures. [2]
After gallbladder removal, the liver continues producing bile, but the body loses the gallbladder's ability to store and release it in response to meals. Bile enters the small intestine more continuously, changing the timing of bile acid exposure and potentially influencing intestinal microbial activity. These changes can alter how bile acids circulate and are metabolized within the digestive system. [3]
For many patients, gallbladder removal successfully addresses their original symptoms. However, others experience persistent or newly developed gastrointestinal complications following surgery. These symptoms are collectively described as Post-Cholecystectomy Syndrome (PCS).
PCS can involve chronic diarrhea, abdominal discomfort, nausea, indigestion, bloating, and recurring pain. A 2025 clinical review reported estimates ranging from 5% to 47% of patients following cholecystectomy. This variation reflects differences in how studies define PCS, which symptoms they include, and how long patients are monitored after surgery. [4]
PCS is not a single disease with one underlying mechanism. Symptoms may result from retained bile duct stones, preexisting gastrointestinal conditions, changes in digestive function, or disturbances in bile acid metabolism. Consequently, identifying the underlying cause is essential when determining an appropriate treatment.
One particularly important complication is post-cholecystectomy diarrhea. A systematic review examining 3,476 patients across 21 studies found that approximately 13.3% developed diarrhea following gallbladder removal. The review also emphasized that bile acid diarrhea should be investigated in patients experiencing persistent postoperative digestive symptoms. [5]
For affected patients, these complications can significantly interfere with ordinary routines. Unpredictable bowel movements, dietary restrictions, and recurring gastrointestinal discomfort can make activities such as eating outside the home, traveling, or attending social events difficult to manage.
Our project focuses on changes in the composition and activity of intestinal bile acids.
Bile acids play an important role in digestion, but they also interact with the intestinal microbiome. The liver produces primary bile acids, including cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA). As these compounds pass through the gastrointestinal tract, intestinal bacteria can modify them into secondary bile acids.
Two important secondary bile acids are:
- Deoxycholic acid (DCA): Produced primarily through the bacterial modification of cholic acid.
- Lithocholic acid (LCA): Produced primarily through the bacterial modification of chenodeoxycholic acid.
These secondary bile acids differ in their chemical structures, biological activities, and interactions with intestinal tissues. LCA is particularly hydrophobic and can exhibit cellular toxicity at certain concentrations. [1, 6]
Following cholecystectomy, changes in bile delivery may alter the environment in which intestinal microorganisms metabolize bile acids. A 2022 study examining patients who had undergone gallbladder removal reported increased fecal concentrations of secondary bile acids, including LCA and DCA, compared with healthy controls. [6]
Earlier research also demonstrated that cholecystectomy can influence the conversion of primary bile acids into secondary bile acids. In a study of nine women, the proportion of cholic acid converted into DCA increased following surgery. However, the overall DCA pool did not increase because the synthesis of its precursor decreased. [3]
These findings suggest that gallbladder removal can change intestinal bile acid metabolism, but they do not establish that every patient develops excessive LCA or that LCA is responsible for every case of PCS.
Excessive concentrations of certain bile acids in the colon can contribute to digestive problems by stimulating intestinal water and electrolyte secretion. This process is an established mechanism behind bile acid diarrhea. [7]
LCA also presents an interesting target because of its chemical structure and potential toxicity. However, LCA and other secondary bile acids have important signaling functions within the body. Some studies have demonstrated protective effects of secondary bile acids in experimental inflammatory models. [6]
For RENATA, this creates an important engineering consideration: the objective is to investigate the selective modification of LCA, not eliminate all bile acids from the intestine.
An intervention that changes bile acid metabolism must account for the digestive and signaling functions of these molecules. Our project therefore investigates whether sulfation, a naturally occurring biochemical modification, could provide a way to alter the properties of LCA.
Treatment for PCS depends on the underlying cause of a patient's symptoms. Structural complications may require endoscopic or surgical intervention, and some patients benefit from dietary changes or treatment for other gastrointestinal conditions.
For patients with bile acid diarrhea, physicians commonly prescribe medications known as bile acid sequestrants. These include cholestyramine, colestipol, and colesevelam. [7]
Cholestyramine is one of the most established treatments for bile acid diarrhea. It works by binding bile acids within the intestinal tract, reducing their ability to stimulate fluid secretion in the colon. This can decrease diarrhea and improve symptoms in patients whose condition is driven by excessive colonic bile acid exposure.
However, several limitations can make cholestyramine difficult to use consistently.
Patients may experience constipation, bloating, nausea, and abdominal discomfort. Cholestyramine can also interfere with the absorption of certain oral medications and fat-soluble vitamins, requiring additional consideration when coordinating treatment with other prescriptions. [7, 8]
Long-term adherence presents another challenge. A review of bile acid diarrhea treatment reported that 60–70% of patients in the cited clinical populations discontinued cholestyramine or colestipol within five years because of adverse effects. These findings are not specific to every PCS patient, but they demonstrate the practical limitations of existing bile acid sequestrants. [7]
Other medications, including colesevelam, may be better tolerated by some patients. Effective treatments already exist, and their benefits must be considered when evaluating any proposed alternative.
Existing bile acid sequestrants primarily work by physically binding bile acids. RENATA is investigating a different mechanism: enzymatically modifying a selected bile acid to change its chemical properties.
Our proposed approach uses sulfation to increase the polarity of LCA, potentially reducing its intestinal absorption and facilitating elimination.
The goal is to investigate whether this molecular modification could eventually provide a more targeted approach to managing bile acid-related complications.
RENATA's proposed solution centers on SulFlux, our engineered version of a bacterial sulfotransferase intended to recognize and sulfate lithocholic acid.
Sulfotransferases are enzymes that transfer a sulfonate group from a donor molecule to another compound. One important example is the human enzyme SULT2A1, which naturally participates in bile acid sulfation.
Research has demonstrated that sulfation generally increases bile acid water solubility, decreases intestinal absorption, and promotes excretion. Sulfated bile acids are also generally less toxic than their unsulfated counterparts. [9]
Human SULT2A1 is particularly relevant to our project because it can sulfate LCA. A study examining the activity of human SULT2A1 against several bile acids found that LCA exhibited the highest affinity among the bile acids tested. [10]
This provided RENATA with a biological basis for investigating sulfation as a potential strategy for modifying LCA.
Our project builds on research involving Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, a bacterium commonly associated with the human gut microbiome.
In 2022, Yao and colleagues identified a bacterial sulfotransferase, BtSULT, encoded by the gene BT0416. The researchers demonstrated that BtSULT could sulfate certain steroidal metabolites and identified associated genes involved in producing the sulfate donor required for its activity. [11]
However, native BtSULT has an important limitation.
The enzyme preferentially recognizes steroidal molecules with relatively flat ring structures. Ordinary LCA possesses a different, bent molecular configuration that is not efficiently accommodated by the native enzyme.
This substrate preference became the central protein-engineering challenge for RENATA.
Our team proposed modifying regions of BtSULT involved in substrate recognition, drawing on structural differences between bacterial BtSULT and human sulfotransferases capable of processing LCA. The intended result is SulFlux, an engineered enzyme with improved compatibility with LCA.
The purpose of this modification is to investigate whether the enzyme can recognize LCA and catalyze its conversion into a sulfated derivative.
SulFlux remains an engineered research design whose activity must be experimentally established. Its proposed function is based on known sulfotransferase chemistry and our structural engineering rationale.
For SulFlux to carry out the intended sulfation reaction, it requires a sulfate donor known as 3′-phosphoadenosine-5′-phosphosulfate (PAPS).
PAPS supplies the sulfonate group transferred by sulfotransferase enzymes. Without sufficient availability of this cofactor, an otherwise functional sulfotransferase may be unable to sustain the intended reaction.
The research identifying BtSULT also described a bacterial gene cluster containing the machinery needed to produce PAPS. This includes BT0413–BT0415, which encode enzymes involved in PAPS biosynthesis. [11]
RENATA's proposed design incorporates a PAPS-production component alongside SulFlux, allowing us to investigate the relationship between sulfate-donor availability and the intended enzymatic activity.
Our design also considers the metabolic burden associated with expressing multiple biological components within the same cell. We explored gene-expression regulation as a way to manage cellular resource demands, including the possibility of controlling expression in response to relevant intestinal conditions.
These components form the basis of our proposed biological system.
An important part of our research is determining whether SulFlux can perform the intended biochemical transformation.
Our proposed evaluation focuses on detecting sulfotransferase activity and establishing whether LCA is converted into a sulfated product. We also considered methods for assessing protein expression and distinguishing successful biochemical conversion from other reactions occurring within the experimental system.
Demonstrating general sulfotransferase activity would represent an initial step, but it would not independently establish successful LCA modification. Confirming the identity of the sulfated product and evaluating the enzyme's selectivity are necessary for validating the proposed mechanism.
These questions guide the experimental development of SulFlux and provide criteria for evaluating whether further engineering is justified.
The long-term objective of RENATA is to investigate whether SulFlux could be incorporated into an engineered probiotic system intended to operate within the gastrointestinal tract.
Probiotic-based delivery is of interest because certain microorganisms can survive passage through parts of the digestive system and carry out biochemical functions within the intestinal environment.
Our proposed system would use an appropriate bacterial chassis to express SulFlux, with the longer-term goal of modifying LCA locally in the gut. Our laboratory design also considered bacterial expression systems for evaluating the engineered enzyme before any potential application in a probiotic organism.
Through our Human Practices research, we also examined the practical limitations patients experience with existing digestive treatments. Patient interviews and online responses repeatedly brought up concerns about medication convenience, dietary restrictions, and unwanted side effects.
These considerations contributed to our exploration of a prebiotic-probiotic formulation, intended to make a future treatment easier to incorporate into patients' daily routines.
A prebiotic component could potentially support the intended microbial system, but its necessity and effectiveness would require separate evaluation. Likewise, using a probiotic delivery strategy does not automatically establish safety, tolerability, or clinical effectiveness.
Our proposed therapeutic system would need to demonstrate that the engineered bacteria could perform the intended reaction under relevant intestinal conditions without introducing unacceptable biological risks.
Post-Cholecystectomy Syndrome presents a challenge because patients can develop similar gastrointestinal symptoms through different underlying mechanisms. For patients whose symptoms involve disturbed bile acid metabolism, modifying specific bile acid metabolites may offer an avenue for future treatment development.
RENATA's project investigates this possibility through SulFlux, an engineered bacterial sulfotransferase intended to convert lithocholic acid into a more water-soluble sulfated derivative. Our design combines the proposed enzyme with consideration of PAPS availability, gene regulation, and eventual probiotic-based delivery.
The scientific challenge begins with substrate recognition. Native BtSULT does not efficiently sulfate ordinary LCA, requiring us to investigate whether protein engineering can alter its substrate preference. Establishing successful LCA sulfation would provide evidence for the biochemical feasibility of our approach.
Further research would still be required to evaluate the selectivity of SulFlux, the stability and biological effects of its reaction products, and its influence on the wider intestinal bile acid pool. Any proposed bacterial delivery system would also require extensive safety and efficacy testing before it could be considered for clinical use.
Through this project, we hope to contribute to research on engineered microbial metabolism and the potential applications of sulfotransferases in gastrointestinal biotechnology. Our immediate objective is to establish the feasibility of the proposed biochemical mechanism and identify the challenges that must be addressed before it could advance toward a therapeutic application.
